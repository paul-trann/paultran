Automated Snack Dispenser

IoT-enabled dispenser using sensors and actuators for precise automated control.

+

Overview

College students often struggle to find convenient, portion-controlled snack options due to busy schedules. While buying snacks in bulk saves money, eating directly from large bags can lead to overeating and poor portion awareness. This project demonstrates an automatic dispenser using coffee beans as an example. The system efficiently grinds and dispenses single servings of snacks—like candy or crackers - helping users save time, maintain portion control, and build healthier habits without sacrificing the benefits of bulk buying.

What I Did

  • We used two sensors connected to an ESP32 microcontroller: an ultrasonic sensor mounted on top to measure remaining snack levels, and a light sensor that detects when a cup is placed by sensing a drop in light. These sensors trigger two actuators—a set of LEDs that indicate snack levels (green = full, yellow = half, red = refill needed), and a servo motor that dispenses a single serving when a cup is detected.
  • We designed a compact dispensing unit (10.5" × 6.5" × 6.5") to fit a cup or small bowl for snack collection. The plywood frame includes precisely measured slots for the servo motor and ultrasonic sensor, with a dedicated housing for the ESP32 microcontroller to reduce light interference and improve sensor accuracy. The dispensing mechanism combines a servo motor with gravity-assisted flow for accurate portioning and minimal spillage. A rubber band at the exit point balances force, reducing strain on the motor and enhancing stability.
  • In this project, my ESP32 acted as the receiver, communicating with a primary ESP32 via its MAC address. I programmed it to trigger the servo motor when sensor conditions were met, dispensing a single serving in 0.35 seconds for precise portion control. To counteract tension from a rubber band in the hatch mechanism, the servo was powered through the 5V pin for smooth operation.

Results

Dispensed consistent portions within about 5% of the target weight.

Problems Faced

  • Problem: Component issues led to a weak light sensor that required a flashlight. Solution: We added a cutout to let more light into the housing to accommodate the poor sensor.
  • Problem: The door allowing the food to fall was attached to the servo alone, which could not support all the weight. Solution: We added a rubber band to balance the load across the axle

iPhone LiDAR Mount

Designed and fabricated a LiDAR-enabled measurement mount using CNC, waterjet, and 3D printing.

+

Overview

While LiDAR-equipped smartphones offer powerful 3D scanning capabilities, handheld use often leads to motion inconsistencies, incomplete coverage, and user fatigue. The LiDAR iPhone Mount solves this by providing a stable, controlled scanning setup for organic objects. With a rotating bearing, secure mount, adjustable arm, and counterweight, it enables smooth 360° rotation and accurate depth capture. This improves model precision, reduces manual error, and makes high-quality mobile scanning more accessible for research and creative applications.

What I Did

  • The design centers on an 80mm bearing and a custom 3D-printed iPhone mount optimized for LiDAR scanning of small organic objects. A stable base plate and rigid steel–aluminum structure minimize distortion during operation. The PLA mount allows precise customization while remaining lightweight, and the entire assembly is secured with fasteners. To ensure smooth rotation, the bearing sleeve is press-fit into the frame, supported by spacers and a reinforced base for stability. The iPhone is held securely on one end of the rotating arm, while a counterweight on the opposite end balances the system, enabling accurate, uninterrupted 360° scans.
  • Precision was essential in fabricating the LiDAR iPhone mount. I waterjet-cut the metal components for clean edges and accurate dimensions, then refined them on a CNC milling machine for optimal fit. The bearing sleeve required multiple lathe passes to achieve a precise press fit, which is tight enough for stability and smooth enough for rotation. Final assembly used fasteners and spacers to ensure durability and motion accuracy.

Arcade Machine

Designed a gantry system with 5 belts and 4 rails; programmed servo motors for motion control.

+

Overview

The Lily Pad Plunge’s purpose is to bring the old-time arcade experience into your home. The game will have up, down, left, and right controls along with a drop button. The machine will have a string and a pulley that holds a 3D-printed frog. We will use the controls to position the frog above the lily pads on the machine's bottom. The goal is to drop the frog on the lily pad. The smaller the lily pad is, the more points it is worth. Each turn will end after a set amount of time has passed.

What I Did

  • We designed a gantry system with smooth motion across three axes (X, Y, Z), using a cross-shaped frame mounted to belts for controlled movement. The machine’s frame is a compact 12" × 12" × 24" box made from ¼" plywood, scaled down from traditional arcade machines for portability and home use. The console housing includes directional buttons and an LCD screen for real- time gameplay feedback, with space below for electrical components. To avoid adhesives that could damage parts, we used interference fits to secure the buttons and screen — ensuring durability and clean assembly.
  • To build Lily Pad Plunge, we began by laser-cutting and assembling the housing components. We programmed two servo motors to control the gantry system and three additional motors to operate the conveyor belt carrying the lily pads. After configuring the gantry for precise frog movement and drop accuracy, we integrated both systems into the game’s frame. We then organized the electrical components, tested the LCD and buttons for real-time gameplay feedback, and mounted the controls using a microcontroller and breadboard. An attempt counter was added to limit each player to three turns, allowing smooth transitions between users. Finally, we painted the game, made final adjustments, and presented it to our club — where it now lives as a daily-use arcade piece.
